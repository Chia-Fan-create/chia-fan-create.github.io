TIA Robot: a transit report generator, built twice

Every traffic impact assessment starts with the same chores: find the bus stops near a site, list the routes, check service frequency, pull bike-share data, and turn it all into charts.

At THI I led development of an internal tool that pulled data from Taiwan's open government transit APIs and generated the dashboards, charts, maps, and report summaries automatically. The code stayed with the company when I left, so I rebuilt it from scratch in Pittsburgh using Pittsburgh Regional Transit's GTFS feed and POGOH bike-share data from WPRDC. Enter a coordinate and a radius, and it returns stop maps, route tables, a service-frequency heatmap, and bike-share usage in about 10 to 20 seconds.

The original version reduced manual reporting effort by 80%. The Pittsburgh version runs end to end on public data, and the code is open.

A collision diagram system for 22 local governments

Traffic crashes were a major public concern in Taiwan, and the government wanted to use collision diagrams to find contributing factors and fix dangerous intersections.

I coordinated the development of a central management system and the process around it. Talking with government stakeholders, we found two different needs: the central government had to track projects and funding, while local governments needed tools to draw collision diagrams and manage their intersection improvements.

So I helped define what each level of government was responsible for and proposed a two-level approach. The central government got a management platform that tracks project progress and cuts down manual Excel reporting. Local governments got a funding process to build their own platforms, with the features each of them needed. Throughout, I worked between the agencies and our technical team to keep the system aligned with how people actually do their jobs.

The central management platform was delivered on time, and local governments moved ahead with their own systems. What I took from it: a system succeeds when it fits its users' needs, clear responsibilities, and daily work, not only when the technology works.

Related: Street Doctor, our 2023 Presidential Hackathon entry, proposed digitizing accident data, analyzing safety indicators, showing real-time 3D insights, and building a public warning system. It was named an Excellence Team, one of the top 4 of 154 teams. Its full name, the Tiger Mouth Extraction Project, comes from a Mandarin phrase: a "tiger's mouth" is a dangerous spot, and the project set out to pull its teeth.

SmartCart: grocery prices you can actually compare

A 12-pack and a 30-pack can't be compared on sticker price. Amazon, Target, and Walmart all list the same products in different sizes and units.

I proposed the idea and led the React front end: the pages, the API integration, and the interface. The three of us designed the rest together, including a normalized 15-table MySQL schema with indexes, transactions, functions, and triggers, and the cleaning logic that converts inconsistent units into a true price per ounce, roll, or count.

The finished app compares unit prices across all three retailers and also tracks shopping lists, household inventory, spending trends, and price alerts.

Methods

Hazmat routes with no single right answer

A route for a truck carrying hazardous materials has to balance three goals that pull against each other: operating cost, accident risk, and how quickly emergency responders could reach an incident.

I implemented the NSGA-II evolutionary algorithm in Python to search across all three objectives at once. Instead of one "optimal" route, it returns the set of Pareto-optimal alternatives, which I visualized so decision makers could see exactly what each option trades away.

Related models were published in the Journal of the Eastern Asia Society for Transportation Studies and presented at the 2017 Chinese Institute of Transportation annual conference.

Machine learning practice

  • CIFAR-10 image generation with a DCGAN. Trained an unconditional DCGAN in PyTorch on all 50,000 CIFAR-10 training images across 10 classes, using one-sided label smoothing and tracking both losses at every one of 19,550 iterations. By epoch 25 the samples showed horses on grass, ships on water, and planes in the sky, with no mode collapse. My analysis explains why they stay blurry: ten diverse classes at 32×32, a small four-layer model trained for 50 epochs, a discriminator that slowly gained the upper hand, and no spectral normalization, gradient penalty, or class conditioning.

  • Rainfall prediction classifier. Predicts next-day rain from the Rain in Australia dataset, with feature engineering, a class-balance check, a preprocessing pipeline, and a comparison of a grid-searched random forest against logistic regression. Code on GitHub, Notebook on Kaggle
  • Titanic survival prediction. Random forest and logistic regression tuned with cross-validated grid search; Kaggle score 0.7799. Code on GitHub

About

What I like about transportation is that it rarely has a standard answer. Every road, every intersection, tells a slightly different story.

That's why, as a civil engineering student, I went a different way from most of my classmates. They went into engineering; I went to National Cheng Kung University to study transportation management, the side of the field where policy, operations, and people's daily routines meet. Transportation touches almost everyone's day, and good management can make a real difference to a whole community.

At THI Consultants I spent five years on exactly those problems: crash-prone intersections, opening-day traffic at a new outlet mall, the roads around a semiconductor fab. Along the way I kept reaching for code to take repetitive work off my team's plate, and I came to Pitt to get better at it. I like learning new tools when they help solve a real-world problem.

The work I care about most either matters to society, like a safer crossing for a whole county, or makes a real difference at a smaller scale, like a tool that gives a team its afternoons back.

Outside work I run. I've done half marathons in Taipei, the Pittsburgh relay marathon this year, and the Philadelphia Half is next. I also practice yoga, lift, cook, keep a journal, and shoot film. Most of the photos and video on this site are mine.
